Rehearsal that works, and the questions after

Reading your notes in your head is not rehearsal; it is proofreading. A talk changes only when you say it out loud, at full length, against a clock, and listen back. Tonight: rehearsal you can measure with the session 4 bench, the questions you can write down before anyone asks them, what to say when you do not know, and the run-through that ends the preparation so the talk can begin.

Part 1 · covers rehearsal

Out loud, standing, on the clock

6 min live

A talk read silently runs faster and smoother than the same talk said aloud, because the mouth finds every long sentence and every word you cannot pronounce at speed. Rehearsal that changes a talk has four parts: say it out loud, at full length, time it, and listen back to a recording. Everything else is reading.

LiveWhat counts as a rehearsal3 min▶

Four conditions, and a run missing any of them is preparation, not rehearsal:

  • Out loud. At the volume you will use in the room. Muttering hides the long sentences, because a mutter does not need breath.
  • Full length, no stopping. When you stumble, recover the way you will have to on the day: pause, find your place on the card, carry on. Stopping to restart trains the restart.
  • On the clock. Against the budget from session 1. A talk that runs over in rehearsal runs further over in the room.
  • Recorded. Your memory of a run is kinder than the recording. The recording is also what the bench measures.

Standing helps if you will stand on the day, because breath and pace change when you are upright. If the talk is seated or on a call, rehearse seated.

LiveTest it on someone who was not in your head2 min▶

Chris Anderson's third guideline ends with a rehearsal instruction: "It's important, therefore, to test your talk on trusted friends, and find out which parts they get confused by." The useful question to ask your test listener is not "was it good?", which gets politeness, but "where did you get lost?" and "what was it arguing?". If their answer to the second is not your session 1 sentence, the talk has drifted and no amount of delivery practice will fix it.

Self-studyHow many rehearsals, honestly2 min▶

There is no measured number of rehearsals that makes a talk ready, and any figure you see quoted without a study behind it is a habit, not a finding. A working stopping rule instead: rehearse until two runs in a row land inside your time budget and the bench shows nothing you would fix. For most ten-minute talks that is a handful of full runs, but the rule is the stopping condition, not the count. Rehearse the first thirty seconds and the last line more than the middle, because those are the parts said under the most adrenaline (session 3) and the parts the room remembers (session 2).

Real world

Noor's first full run (constructed) took 13 minutes 40 seconds for a ten-minute slot. The recording showed why: she re-explained the six-day wait twice because she had not decided where it went. The fix was one decision, not faster speech: the story lives in support 1 and is referred to, not retold, in support 3. Her second run came in at 10:20 and her third at 8:50, inside the budget with more than a minute of buffer.

Part 2 · covers the questions

Most questions can be written down the day before

5 min live

Questions feel unpredictable from the stage and are almost entirely predictable from a desk. Each support in your talk raises the same few questions, from the same few people. Writing them down the day before turns the scariest part of a talk into the best-prepared part, and session 2 already put the questions before your last line, so the talk still ends on your sentence.

LiveThe question list3 min▶

For each of your three supports, ask who in the room is most affected by it and what they will want to know. Three voices cover most of it: the skeptic ("how do you know it was the order and not something else?"), the person whose number moves ("so my weekly report gets worse?"), and the senior person ("what do you need from us, and what does it cost?"). Write each question in their words, then a one-sentence answer, then the line that connects the answer back to your throughline. Six questions is usually enough; if you cannot answer one in a sentence, that support is not finished.

LiveThe question you cannot answer2 min▶

Say so, say when you will know, and do it in front of them: "I don't know the exact number for the northern queue. I'll check and send it to everyone by Friday." Then write it down where the room can see you do it. The commitment is what makes "I don't know" sound prepared rather than caught out. The move that costs you is the confident guess, because a wrong number said from the stage is repeated by the people who heard it and has to be corrected in writing later, to more people.

Self-studyInterruptions, workshops and rooms that talk back2 min▶

Questions in the middle of a meeting-length message, where interrupting is normal and is usually the real question, are covered in Communication, session 5 ↗. If your talk is really a session where the room is meant to do the talking, that is facilitation, a different craft taught in Facilitation ↗. An all-hands talk sits in between: questions after, at a time you announced, with the last word yours.

Real world

Noor's list (constructed) had six questions, and four were asked. The one she had flagged as hardest, from the finance lead, was "does the two-day number go in the board pack, or only the average?". She had a one-sentence answer ready: both, side by side, for one quarter, then the board decides. The question she had not predicted, about the northern queue's reopen rate, got "I don't know the exact figure; I'll send it by Friday", written on the whiteboard as she said it.

Part 3 · covers the last run-through

The day before, and the hour before

4 min live

At some point preparation has to stop, or it turns into rewriting the talk the night before and walking on with a version you have said once. The final run-through is the line: a full rehearsal, with the slides, on the clock, after which the content is frozen and only delivery is allowed to change.

LiveFreeze the content2 min▶

A late rewrite feels like improvement and arrives on stage as the least-rehearsed part of the talk, usually in the middle where you are most likely to lose your place. After the final run, keep a list of changes you want to make, and do not make them. If one is genuinely essential (a number turned out wrong), change that sentence, say the paragraph around it out loud three times, and leave everything else alone.

Self-studyThe hour before2 min▶

Practical, not inspirational: see the room, stand where you will stand, click through the slides on the actual machine, find the water, check the time display. Put the card in the pocket you will reach for. Say the first sentence once, quietly, and the reappraisal line from session 3 if it helps you. Then stop preparing. The talk is ready; the hour is for the room.

Real world

The night before (constructed), Noor wanted to add the regional numbers back in. The change list said no: they were in the one-page note, the note was ready to send, and the frozen run had come in at 8:50 with the buffer intact. She sent the note at the end of the talk instead, and the two people who asked about regions already had it in their inbox.

Build-along

Rehearse once, measure it, fix one thing

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Six steps on your own talk, with Noor's constructed version alongside. You need your card from session 1, your opening and close from session 2, your slides from session 5, a phone to record with, and the session 4 bench open in another tab. If the full talk is longer than ten minutes, rehearse the opening and the first support tonight and the rest at home.

One full run, out loud, recorded and timed. Stand if you will stand. Do not stop when you stumble; recover and carry on. Note the total time and the time at the end of each support.

★ Step 1 · the runbudget (session 1): open 0:45 | supports 2:30 each | close 0:45 | buffer 1:00 Noor, run 1 (constructed): 13:40 total, support 1 ended at 5:10 yours: ____ total, support 1 ended at ____

Measure the opening on the bench. Transcribe the first two minutes (any phone or meeting app), paste it into the session 4 bench with its exact duration, and copy the four numbers.

★ Step 2 · the numberspace (wpm) | words per sentence | pause marks per 100 | fillers (real ones) Noor, after the cut (rung 2): 136.6 | 15.9 | 13.8 | 1 yours: ____ | ____ | ____ | ____

Pick the single worst spot and fix only that. The overrun, the longest sentence, the cluster of fillers, or the support that got retold. One fix per round, then run again.

★ Step 3 · one fixNoor's worst spot: retold the six-day wait in support 3 (overrun 3:40) fix: support 3 refers back ("that customer") instead of retelling yours: worst spot ________________ fix ________________

Write the question list. Two questions per support, in the asker's words: the skeptic, the person whose number moves, the senior person. A one-sentence answer for each, and the line that ties it back to your throughline.

★ Step 4 · six questionsQ (finance lead): "Does the two-day number go in the board pack?" A: "Both, side by side for one quarter, then the board decides." back to the line: "Because the average alone is what hid the six days." yours: six rows, same three parts

Write your "I don't know" line and say it out loud. A date, a channel, and the action of writing it down. It should sound as rehearsed as your opening, because it is.

★ Step 5 · the honest answer"I don't know the exact figure for ________. I'll check and send it to everyone by ________." then: write it on the board or in your notes, where they can see

Make the final card. Front: throughline, opening line, three supports with one number each, the ask, "questions, then the last line". Back: the three questions you most expect, with a two- or three-word cue for each answer.

★ Step 6 · the cardFRONT sentence | open | 1 ____ 2 ____ 3 ____ | ask | questions, then last line BACK Q1 ____ : cue | Q2 ____ : cue | Q3 ____ : cue | unknown: "by Friday"

Run it once more before you sleep on it. If the second run lands inside the budget and the bench shows nothing you would change, the talk is ready. Freeze it, and spend the rest of the preparation on the room.

Homework

Where to go from here

30 min
  1. Run the talk twice more before the day, each recorded and timed, one fix per run. Stop when two runs in a row fit the budget.
  2. Test it on one person outside your team and ask only "where did you get lost?" and "what was it arguing?".
  3. Give the talk. Afterwards, write three lines: what the room asked that you predicted, what it asked that you did not, and one thing you would cut next time.
  4. Re-run the session 4 bench on a recording of the real talk if you can get one, and compare it with your first run-through. That pair of numbers is your evidence that preparation changed something.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 6 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Which of these is a rehearsal rather than preparation?

Out loud, full length, timed and recorded: each condition finds something the others miss. A and C skip the breath and the clock; B adds unrehearsed material at the worst possible moment.

2 · The finance lead asks for a number you do not have. What is the strongest answer?

A commitment with a date makes "I don't know" sound prepared. B creates a wrong number that travels; C and D leave the question hanging, and the room notices.

3 · The night before, you think of a better example for support 2. What does the freeze rule say?

A late change is the least-rehearsed part of the talk and it lands in the middle, where losing your place costs most. B also breaks the time budget from session 1.
