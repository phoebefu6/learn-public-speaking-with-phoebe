One idea worth the room

Most first talks fail before anyone stands up. They fail at the desk, when the speaker tries to fit everything they know into ten minutes and the room leaves with nothing in particular. Tonight: why a talk can carry one idea and not three, how to find yours in one sentence, how to cut everything that does not serve it, and how much a ten-minute slot really holds.

Part 0

Where we start

Six sessions, one running case. Noor Haddad is a constructed team lead: she runs a nine-person support operations team at a mid-sized online retailer, and she has been given ten minutes at next month's all-hands to explain a change her team is making. It is her first talk to the whole company. Noor, her team, her numbers and her transcripts are invented for teaching, and every page says so. Alongside her you work on a talk of your own, starting tonight with the hardest and least visible part: deciding what it is about.

Part 1 · covers the throughline

A talk carries one idea

6 min live

A reader can go back. A listener cannot. Three topics in one talk feel generous to the speaker and arrive as three half-explained things, and each listener keeps whichever one happened to land. The working fix has a name in TED's own guidance: the throughline, one idea that every part of the talk connects back to.

LiveThe throughline, in the words of the person who named it3 min▶

Chris Anderson, who runs TED, gave four guidelines in his 2016 talk "TED's secret to great public speaking". The first is the one this session is built on: "limit your talk to just one major idea". He goes on to say you have to "slash back your content" to the single idea you care about most, and to make it "the through-line running through your entire talk, so that everything you say links back to it in some way."

His other three guidelines, in order: give the audience a reason to care before you start building (session 2 picks this up as the opening); build the idea out of concepts the audience already understands; and ask honestly who the idea benefits. The second and third matter for an all-hands: your colleagues do not share your team's vocabulary, so the throughline has to be said in theirs.

One honest caveat. Anderson is describing what works at TED, a stage with rehearsal coaching and a curious audience that chose to be there. An all-hands has a captive room, a shorter slot and a boss in the front row. The one-idea rule transfers well because it is about the listener's memory, not the venue. The production values do not, and the rest of these sessions do not assume them.

Self-studyWhy "three updates" is the default, and why it fails3 min▶

An update is organised the way the work was done: first we did this, then that, then the tooling. That order is natural for the person who did the work and useless for the person hearing about it, because it answers "what happened" when the room is asking "what does it mean for me". The three parts get equal time because they took equal effort, not because they matter equally.

The listener cannot fix this for you. A reader who loses the thread skims back to the heading. A listener who loses it waits for a signal that the topic has changed, and in a three-topic talk that signal arrives twice, each time resetting what they were holding.

Structuring a written or spoken message by what the reader needs first, the ask-first skeletons, is taught next door in Communication, session 3 ↗. This page is about the talk-sized version: one idea that survives ten minutes of a room's attention.

Real world

Ken Robinson's 2006 talk "Do schools kill creativity?" says its throughline out loud, early. In the published transcript he says "My contention is that creativity now is as important in education as literacy", and then spends the rest of the talk on stories that hang from that one claim. The line is short, it is a claim someone could disagree with, and every story after it is evidence for it. That is the shape tonight's build-along is aiming at.

Part 2 · covers the one-sentence test and the cut

If you cannot say it in one sentence, the room cannot either

5 min live

The throughline is not the topic. "The ticket queue" is a topic. "From Monday we answer the customer who has waited longest first" is a throughline: it is a claim, it is short enough to repeat, and someone in the room could disagree with it. The test is mechanical: write it as one sentence, then run everything you planned to say past it.

LiveWhat makes a sentence a throughline3 min▶

Three checks, each one you can run on paper:

  • It is a claim, not a label. "Our Q3 results" is a label. "Q3 grew because two clients doubled, and that will not repeat" is a claim. A label tells the room what you will talk about; a claim tells them what to think when you are done.
  • It is said in the room's words. "Moving to FIFO on the L1 queue" is Noor's team's vocabulary. "The customer who has waited longest goes first" is everyone's. Anderson's third guideline is exactly this: build from concepts the audience already has.
  • It survives being repeated by someone else. Ask a colleague from another team to say back what your talk is about after hearing only the sentence. If what comes back is a different sentence, the room will do the same thing at scale.
LiveThe cut list, and where cut material goes2 min▶

Run every item you planned to say past the sentence and sort it three ways: serves it (keep, it becomes a support), true but not this talk (cut to the handout or the follow-up note), serves me, not the room (cut entirely: the history of how hard it was, the credit list, the caveats nobody asked about).

The cut is the step speakers skip, because cut material feels like lost work. Writing it down as a list, with a destination, is what makes it possible. Noor's regional breakdown is real and interesting; it goes in a one-page note sent after the talk, where people who want it can read it at their own speed.

Self-studyWhen you genuinely have two ideas2 min▶

Sometimes the slot really is for two things: a change and a request for budget, say. Two honest options. Either find the sentence that contains both ("we are changing the order, and it needs one more hire to hold") so the second becomes a support of the first, or give two talks: ask for a second slot, or put one of them in writing. What does not work is two throughlines in ten minutes, because each gets five, and five minutes is about one support.

Real world

Anderson's own talk follows his rule. It is under eight minutes long (475 seconds in the public TED dataset), and its single idea is the one in its first lines: a speaker's "number one task" is to transfer an idea into the listeners' minds. Everything after, the four guidelines included, is a support for that sentence. He did not try to also cover lighting, slides and stage fright.

Part 3 · covers how much a slot holds

Ten minutes holds about three supports

4 min live

A time slot is a word budget. Session 4 measures the pace of 2,299 one-speaker TED talks from their transcripts and running times: the median is 152 words a minute over the whole talk, applause included. At that pace a ten-minute slot holds about 1,500 words. Spend them before you write them.

LiveWhat 380 words buys2 min▶

Three hundred and eighty words is about one page of double-spaced text. Said out loud it holds one example told properly, one number said twice (the spoken version repeats the number that matters, as Communication, session 5 ↗ teaches), and one sentence that hooks the support back to the throughline. It does not hold a support plus its history plus three caveats.

The buffer is not optional. Talks run long when they meet a room: a laugh, a slide that will not advance, a question shouted from the back. A talk written to exactly ten minutes runs to twelve.

Self-studyWhy the median and not a "correct" pace2 min▶

There is no correct pace, and session 4 shows why: across the 2,299 talks the middle half ran between 137 and 168 words a minute, over the whole running time. The median is used here only to turn minutes into a budget. If you know you speak at 130, budget at 130; the bench in session 4 will tell you your own number from a recording. The budget is about planning what fits, not about speaking at any particular speed.

Real world

Noor's first plan (constructed) was 2,400 words. At her rehearsed pace that is more than fifteen minutes for a ten-minute slot. The cut list took out the regional breakdown, the reopen pattern, the tooling roadmap and the history since March, about 1,300 words between them, and the first three became a one-page note sent the same afternoon. By its third rehearsal (session 6) the talk that remained fit with more than a minute to spare, and the note was read by the three people who needed the detail.

Build-along

Find the one sentence, on your own talk

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Six steps on a talk you have to give or one you gave recently, with Noor's constructed answers alongside as the worked example. Work in a plain document. Pen and paper is fine; the only rule is that every step produces something written, because a throughline that exists only in your head has not been tested yet.

Empty your head first. List everything you could say in the talk, one line each, in any order. Do not judge yet. Most people find between eight and fifteen items.

★ Step 1 · the pileNoor (constructed), 8 items: the six-day wait in May regional breakdown the average that hid it reopen pattern what changes on Monday tooling roadmap macro rewrite history since March yours: one line per item, no limit

Write the one sentence. A claim, in the room's words, short enough to say in one breath. If you write a label ("an update on X"), rewrite it as what you want the room to think or do when you finish.

★ Step 2 · the sentencelabel (not yet): "An update on the ticket queue." claim (Noor): "From Monday we answer the customer who has waited longest first." yours: "_____________________________________"

Run the pile past it. Mark each item K (serves the sentence), H (true, goes to the handout), or X (serves you, not the room). Expect more H and X than K.

★ Step 3 · sortK the six-day wait in May K the average that hid it K what changes on Monday H regional breakdown, reopen pattern, tooling roadmap, macro rewrite X history since March (how hard it was; not what the room needs)

Give each K one example and one number. If an item has no example and no number, it is an assertion, and the room will not remember it. If it has five numbers, pick the one you will say twice.

★ Step 4 · supports1 the six-day wait example: one customer, one refund number: six days 2 the average hid it example: the dashboard that week number: under four hours 3 what changes example: the new report line number: customers waiting 2+ days

Budget the slot. Take your real slot length, keep a buffer of about a tenth, and split the rest. Multiply by about 150 words a minute, or your own pace once session 4 has measured it.

★ Step 5 · the budgetslot 10:00 buffer 1:00 open 0:45 close 0:45 three supports x 2:30 = 7:30 words at 152 a minute: open ~114 each support ~380 close ~114 yours: slot ____ buffer ____ supports ____ x ____

Run the say-back test. Tell one person from outside your team only the sentence. Ask them to say, in their own words, what your talk will argue. Write down exactly what they say.

★ Step 6 · say-backNoor's first sentence: "We're moving the L1 queue to FIFO." said back by finance: "Something about the queue system?" rewritten: "From Monday we answer the customer who has waited longest first." said back: "You'll do the oldest tickets first, even the hard ones."

Keep the card. Session 2 writes the first thirty seconds and the last line from it, and session 6 rehearses against it. If the sentence changes later, change the card first and let everything else follow.

Homework

Before session 2

30 min
  1. Run the say-back test on two more people, one of them senior. Keep their exact words; if all three differ, the sentence is not finished.
  2. Write the one-page handout from your H items. It makes the cut painless and it is what you will send after the talk.
  3. Find one talk you liked (any recorded talk or a colleague's) and write its throughline in one sentence. If you cannot, notice what you remember instead.
  4. Record yourself saying the sentence and the three supports in under two minutes, on your phone. Keep the file; session 4 measures it.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 1 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Which of these is a throughline rather than a topic?

C is a claim, in the room's words, that someone could disagree with. A, B and D are labels: they say what the talk is about, not what the room should think when it ends.

2 · Noor's regional breakdown is accurate and interesting but does not serve her sentence. What happens to it?

Cut material needs a destination, not a deletion. C and D spend the buffer and the close on something that does not serve the throughline, which is the three-topic talk coming back in through the side door.

3 · At the median pace measured in session 4 (152 words a minute), roughly how much does each of three supports hold in a ten-minute slot with an opening, a close and a one-minute buffer?

Two and a half minutes at 152.2 words a minute is about 380 words. 1,500 is the whole slot. C is the anti-lever session 4 measures: speaking faster gets more words in and leaves fewer pauses behind.
