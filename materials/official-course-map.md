## Running case (constructed, say so on every page)

**Noor Haddad**, a constructed team lead of a nine-person support operations team at a
mid-sized online retailer. Ten-minute slot at her first company all-hands, next month. The
change she must explain: from Monday her team answers the customer who has waited longest
first ("oldest first"), instead of whoever is free picking the quickest ticket. Her constructed
facts (use exactly these, all constructed):
- One week in May: average reply time under four hours; same week a customer in Lisbon waited
  six days for a refund reply.
- The average will rise for about three weeks; the number she will report from now on is
  "customers waiting more than two days".
- Her ask to the room: "When the average rises next week, ask me about the two-day number instead."
- Her throughline: "From Monday we answer the customer who has waited longest first."
- Supports: (1) the six-day wait, (2) the average that hid it, (3) what changes on Monday.
- Cut to a one-page note: regional breakdown, reopen pattern, tooling roadmap, macro rewrite;
  cut entirely: history since March.
- First plan 2,400 words; cut about 1,300; rehearsed pace 126.6 wpm (bench rung 3).
- Her director asked her to also cover regional numbers and tooling (the anti-lever, rung 4).

## Verified facts and evidence tiers

Tier legend: **AS** = read at source (text opened and quoted), **M** = measured by the bench,
**R** = reported (secondary only), **U** = could not verify / not located.

### Session 1 (Anderson, Robinson)
- AS: Chris Anderson, "TED's secret to great public speaking", TED 2016 transcript. Four guidelines:
  (1) "limit your talk to just one major idea" ... "make it the through-line running through your
  entire talk, so that everything you say links back to it in some way"; (2) give your listeners a
  reason to care (spark curiosity); (3) "build your idea, piece by piece, out of concepts that your
  audience already understands"; (4) "Make your idea worth sharing" ("Who does this idea benefit?").
  Opening line: "Your number one task as a speaker is to transfer into your listeners' minds an
  extraordinary gift". Running time 475 s in the Kaggle CSV, 466 s on ted.com (9 s mismatch).
- R only: Anderson's book suggests a throughline in "no more than 15 words" - NOT in the talk. Do not
  quote as fact; do not use "15 words".
- AS: Ken Robinson, "Do schools kill creativity?", TED 2006 transcript: "My contention is that
  creativity now is as important in education as literacy, and we should treat it with the same status."

### Session 2 (Mehrabian)
- AS: Albert Mehrabian's own page (kaaj.com/psych/smorder.html, Silent Messages listing):
  "Total Liking = 7% Verbal Liking + 38% Vocal Liking + 55% Facial Liking"; derived "from
  experiments dealing with communications of feelings and attitudes (i.e., like-dislike)";
  "Unless a communicator is talking about their feelings or attitudes, these equations are not
  applicable."
- U: the 1967 papers themselves (Mehrabian & Wiener 1967, JPSP 6(1):109-114; Mehrabian & Ferris
  1967, J Consulting Psych 31(3):248-252) could not be opened. Do NOT describe their stimuli
  (single word "maybe", photos) as fact; if mentioned, say "reported as" and keep it to one clause,
  or better, rely only on Mehrabian's own disclaimer.
- Teaching line: the numbers apply to judging whether someone LIKES you when words and tone
  conflict; they do not say a talk's content is 7% of its impact.

### Session 3 (nerves)
- AS: Brooks, A. W. (2014). Get excited: Reappraising pre-performance anxiety as excitement. JEP:
  General 143(3), 1144-1158. doi:10.1037/a0035325. Pilot + four studies:
  - Pilot (MTurk, N=300; scenario: a thirty-minute keynote to a 500-person company incl. the CEO): when asked about THEMSELVES (N=153), 90.97% chose "Try to relax and calm down" as the best advice (7.74% "Try to be excited instead of anxious"); same pattern when asked about a coworker. Raters coded 84.94% of open-ended advice as relax/calm down.
  - Study 1 karaoke, N=113: said "I am anxious" / "I am excited" / nothing; singing accuracy
    excited 80.52%, none 69.27%, anxious 52.98%.
  - **Study 2 public speech, N=140**: 2 min to prepare a speech on "why you are a good work
    partner", delivered on camera, told it would be judged by a committee of peers. Said aloud "I am
    excited" vs "I am calm". Three raters blind to condition rated the videos: excited speakers rated
    more persuasive (d=.681), competent (d=.458), confident (d=.705), persistent (d=.505); they spoke
    longer (167 s vs 132 s). Self-reported excitement higher (4.75 vs 4.09). "Self-reported anxiety
    did not differ significantly across conditions" (5.06 vs 5.27).
  - Study 3 math, N=188: "Try to get excited" 3.60 of 8 correct vs calm 2.94 and neutral 2.94;
    heart rate did not differ by condition.
  - Study 4, N=218: threat vs opportunity mind-set mediated the effect.
  - Argument: anxiety and excitement are both high-arousal ("arousal congruent"), so reappraising
    to excitement is easier than calming down (high to low arousal).
- AS: Poynter, M. & Pasqualini, M. S. (2025) (Michelle Poynter and Marcia Smith Pasqualini, Avila University). Getting excited about public speaking: A replication.
  Psychology of Language and Communication 29(1), 254ff. doi:10.58734/plc-2025-0011. Direct
  replication of Study 2, preregistered on OSF (per the authors; OSF record not opened). N=74
  self-report, 71 observer-rated; stopped early (pandemic); mostly women; course credit.
  Self-report matched Brooks (more excited, anxiety unchanged). "None of the observer ratings
  differed significantly between conditions." Authors: no earlier published replication attempt.
  Their sample had .60 to .90 power for Brooks's effect sizes.
- AS: Bosshard, M. & Gomez, P. (2024). Scientific Reports. doi:10.1038/s41598-024-58408-w.
  Meta-analysis of stress-arousal reappraisal / stress mindset: 35 studies, 44 effects, N=5,638;
  pooled d=0.23, d=0.14 after trim-and-fill (CI .04 to .24); Egger's test significant (p=.042).
  Broader than Brooks's self-talk; not confirmed that Brooks is an included study.
- R (abstract only): Ganley et al. (2021) J Numerical Cognition 7:4-19: N=300, four brief
  interventions incl. reappraisal as excitement; "none of the interventions had an effect on
  reports of state anxiety or performance" (math, conceptual test).
- Teach: the self-report finding (feel more excited, anxiety itself unchanged) reproduced; the
  observer-rated performance finding has one direct replication that did not reproduce it, small and
  stopped early. Contested: teach the disagreement. Reappraisal costs nothing and may help; it is not
  a proven performance booster.
- Fear above death:
  - R: 1973 Bruskin Associates survey, reported by the Sunday Times (London), Watson, P. (1973,
    Oct 7), "What people usually fear", p.9: 41% listed "speaking before a group" (reported via
    Dwyer & Davidson 2021). Death reported at 19% (blog secondary only). Whether respondents picked
    several items from a list (blog) or named a greatest fear (Dwyer & Davidson wording) could NOT
    be settled. Reported in The Book of Lists (1977) (not opened).
  - AS: Dwyer, K. K. & Davidson, M. M. (2021). J Education & Educational Development 8(2):255-269
    (ERIC EJ1334965), N=374 students: before a speaking course public speaking and death "were not
    significantly different"; after, death chosen more (170 vs 129).
  - AS: Chapman University Survey of American Fears 2024 (Wave 10), N=1,008, SSRS web panel,
    fielded 21 Mar to 1 Apr 2024: afraid or very afraid of public speaking 29.0%, of dying 31.6%,
    of people they love dying 57.8%.
  - Teaching line: the famous ranking comes from one 1973 market-research survey whose original
    report could not be read; on current data the two fears are close and depend on how you ask.
    Do not repeat "ranks above death" as fact.

### Session 4 (bench) - canon, M (Python reference = node engine to 6 decimals)
- Data: Kaggle "TED Talks" (rounakbanik/ted-talks, v3), "all ... TED Talks uploaded to the
  official TED.com website until September 21st, 2017". Kaggle page licence field says CC BY-NC-SA
  4.0; TED's own usage policy (AS) says TED Talks are CC BY-NC-ND 4.0 and transcript edits are not
  permitted. Resolution: ship NO transcript text, titles or names; only derived per-talk counts.
- 2,550 talk records, 2,467 transcripts, 3 duplicate transcripts removed, 2,464 matched. Kept
  2,299: one speaker, >= 300 words, < 3 music/singing cues. Filmed 1984-2017. Duration field is
  seconds (consistent: Robinson 1164; Anderson 475 vs 466 on ted.com).
- Pace (whole running time, applause included): median 152.2 wpm; p25 136.9; p75 167.7; p1 84.2;
  p5 109.4; p95 191.3; p99 205.7; max 248.0; mean 151.6. Words vs minutes r = 0.922.
- Sentence length median 15.9 words (p25 13.8, p75 18.3). Pause marks median 13.5 per 100 words
  (p25 12.3, p75 14.9). Phrase fillers median 0.19 per 100 words, p95 0.78.
- Hesitations (um uh er erm ah hmm mm): 359 in 4,798,631 words (0.75 per 10,000); 214 talks have any.
  Inference (M): TED transcripts are tidied for reading. TED's transcription guideline on dropping
  fillers is R/U (wiki unreachable) - say "almost certainly edited", cite the measurement.
- Laughter cue in 73.9% of talks, applause cue in 94.9%; median 2 laughter and 1 applause cues.
- Ladder (constructed openings, measured):

- Break (shuffle durations across talks, mulberry32 seed 1 / 4 / 7): median 149.1 / 152.7 / 152.9;

  to "collapse toward the mean"; it does not. The median holds and the spread explodes. Teach what
  it does.
- AS: Sticht, T. G. (1970). Mental aptitude and comprehension of time-compressed and
  compressed-expanded listening selections. J Auditory Research 10:103-109 (ERIC ED066080). 280
  Army inductees; three short military passages (36-56 s); normal 165 wpm; machine-compressed to
  206, 275, 300 wpm. 206 wpm did not differ significantly from its compressed-then-expanded control;
  275 and 300 did (p < .001). Limits: machine compression (not a speaker hurrying), short passages,
  1970 military listeners. The paper also repeats, secondhand, "a typical finding" (Foulke & Sticht
  1969) of little loss up to about 275 wpm - in tension with its own 275 result; Foulke & Sticht 1969
  itself could NOT be opened (U).
- AS (abstract/summary): Wallace (1983) Speed Listening, ERIC ED233306: rates ~180 (normal), 230,
  400, 500 wpm; "Test performance declined as presentation rate increased."

### Session 5 (slides)
- AS: Yue, C. L., Bjork, E. L. & Bjork, R. A. (2013). Reducing verbal redundancy in multimedia
  learning: An undesired desirable difficulty? J Educational Psychology 105(2):266-277.
  doi:10.1037/a0031971. Narrated animated PowerPoint on the life cycle of a star. Exp 1 N=105.
  On-screen text identical to the narration impaired learning; slightly different / abridged text
  did better; "exact correspondence between on-screen text and narration generally impairs
  learning, brief labels within an animation can improve learning." Learners PREFERRED identical text
  (preference and learning disagree). Scope: computer-paced multimedia lessons, students; not a
  study of live talks.
- R: Mayer's redundancy principle as stated by Mayer (Cambridge Handbook) - not opened. Mention
  only as "known in multimedia-learning research as the redundancy principle" without quoting.
- AS: Guy Kawasaki, "The 10/20/30 Rule of PowerPoint", guykawasaki.com, 30 Dec 2005: "a
  PowerPoint presentation should have ten slides, last no more than twenty minutes, and contain no
  font smaller than thirty points." Reasons: "a normal human being cannot comprehend more than ten
  concepts in a meeting"; audiences "read ahead of you because it can read faster than you can
  speak"; alternative font rule: age of the oldest person divided by two. Basis: his experience
  hearing pitches as a venture capitalist; no study cited. = OPINION. (Sibling learn-ai-ppt already
  says "asserted from experience, with no study behind it" - consistent.)

## Not covered (honest)
- Voice training (projection, pitch, breath work as technique), body language and gesture rules,
  stage movement - no read-at-source evidence assembled; not taught beyond a mention.
- Clinical speech anxiety (social anxiety disorder): out of scope; say "if fear stops you speaking
  at all, that is worth a professional's help" without statistics.
- Video-call and hybrid presenting specifics; teleprompters; speaking in a second language
  (see learn-business-english-with-phoebe).
- Comprehension at different live speaking rates (the bench does not measure it).
